INSIDE METAHRISE

How a corporate induction became a VR game 145 employees actually wanted to finish — and how that game ended up in the Malaysia Book of Records.

MetaHRise on-site at MCMC — new hires moving through the induction as a playable VR environment.

Most inductions are a room, a slide deck, and a printed handbook nobody re-reads after week one. MCMC wanted something that would actually stick — so we didn't design a better slideshow. We built a game.

The brief

MCMC's induction was doing what most inductions do: covering the material and losing the room by slide four. New hires walked away with a folder and a vague sense of the org chart, not a feel for the organisation they'd just joined. The ask wasn't "make the deck nicer." It was harder to say out loud and harder to brief: make people want to sit through this.

That's a design problem before it's a technology problem. You can't VR your way out of boring content — you have to change what the person is actually doing while they learn it. Sitting and listening was the thing that had to go.

"You can't VR your way out of boring content. You have to change what the person is doing while they learn it."

— EDT, on the MetaHRise brief
Why a game, specifically

Games teach through consequence, not through delivery. Walk the wrong way in a game and you find out immediately — nobody has to tell you. We built MetaHRise on that principle: new hires move through a VR environment structured around MCMC itself, and the organisation's structure, values and history surface as things you discover by playing, not slides you're told to remember.

The headset was the easy part. The harder design problem was pacing — an induction has to hold someone's attention for long enough to cover real material, without ever feeling like a chore wearing a headset. Every beat in MetaHRise earns the next one; nothing plays just to look impressive in a demo reel.

What we built

MetaHRise plays as a sequence of environments rather than a single space — each one built around a specific part of the induction, so the content changes the room instead of a narrator changing the topic. Progress is visible and immediate: new hires can see how far they are through the experience the same way they'd see progress in any game, which turned out to matter more than we expected. People finish things they can see the end of.

The record

The Malaysia Book of Records entry wasn't the brief — it was a consequence of taking the brief seriously. Once MetaHRise existed as a genuinely playable induction, rather than a VR-flavoured slideshow, it qualified as something worth recognising on its own terms. We'd rather ship the record as a side effect than chase it as the headline.

"We build the technical layer other studios say is too complex, then we ship it. The record was proof, not the point."

— EDT, on shipping MetaHRise
What we'd do differently

Every project teaches you where you over-built. With MetaHRise, the lesson was about scope discipline inside VR specifically — it's easy to keep adding environments because the medium makes everything feel like it deserves one. The strongest sections were the ones we cut hardest, not the ones we added the most detail to. That's the note we carry into every VR training build since.
